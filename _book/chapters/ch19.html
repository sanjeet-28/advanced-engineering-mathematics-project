18  Numerics in General

19 Numerics in General

Numeric analysis or briefly numerics has a distinct flavor that is different from basic calculus, from solving ODEs algebraically, or from other (nonnumeric) areas. Whereas in calculus and in ODEs there were very few choices on how to solve the problem and your answer was an algebraic answer, in numerics you have many more choices and your answers are given as tables of values (numbers) or graphs. You have to make judicious choices as to what numeric method or algorithm you want to use, how accurate you need your result to be, with what value (starting value) do you want to begin your computation, and others. This chapter is designed to provide a good transition from the algebraic type of mathematics to the numeric type of mathematics.

We begin with the general concepts such as floating point, roundoff errors, and general numeric errors and their propagation. This is followed in Sec. 19.2 by the important topic of solving equations of the type \(f(x) = 0\) by various numeric methods, including the famous Newton method. Section 19.3 introduces interpolation methods. These are methods that construct new (unknown) function values from known function values. The knowledge gained in Sec. 19.3 is applied to spline interpolation (Sec. 19.4) and is useful for understanding numeric integration and differentiation covered in the last section.

Numerics provides an invaluable extension to the knowledge base of the problem-solving engineer. Many problems have no solution formula (think of a complicated integral or a polynomial of high degree or the interpolation of values obtained by measurements). In other cases a complicated solution formula may exist but may be practically useless. It is for these kinds of problems that a numerical method may generate a good answer. Thus, it is very important that the applied mathematician, engineer, physicist, or scientist becomes familiar with the essentials of numerics and its ideas, such as estimation of errors, order of convergence, numerical methods expressed in algorithms, and is also informed about the important numeric methods.

Prerequisite: Elementary calculus.
References and Answers to Problems: App. 1 Part E, App. 2.

19.1 Introduction

As an engineer or physicist you may deal with problems in elasticity and need to solve an equation such as \(f(x) = 0\) or a more difficult problem of finding the roots of a higher order polynomial. Or you encounter an integral such as

\[\int_0^1 e^{-x^2} \, dx\]

(see App. 3, formula (35)) that you cannot solve by elementary calculus. Such problems, which are difficult or impossible to solve algebraically, arise frequently in applications. They call for numeric methods, that is, systematic methods that are suitable for solving, numerically, the problems on computers or calculators. Such solutions result in tables of numbers, graphical representation (figures), or both. Typical numeric methods are iterative in nature and, for a well-chosen problem and a good starting value, will frequently converge to a desired answer. The evolution from a given problem that you observed in an experimental lab or in an industrial setting (in engineering, physics, biology, chemistry, economics, etc.) to an approximation suitable for numerics to a final answer usually requires the following steps.

  1. Modeling. We set up a mathematical model of our problem, such as an integral, a system of equations, or a differential equation.

  2. Choosing a numeric method and parameters (e.g., step size), perhaps with a preliminary error estimation.

  3. Programming. We use the algorithm to write a corresponding program in a CAS, such as Maple, Mathematica, Matlab, or Mathcad, or, say, in Java, C, or FORTRAN, selecting suitable routines from a software system as needed.

  4. Doing the computation.

  5. Interpreting the results in physical or other terms, also deciding to rerun if further results are needed.

Steps 1 and 2 are related. A slight change of the model may often admit of a more efficient method. To choose methods, we must first get to know them. Chapters 19–21 contain efficient algorithms for the most important classes of problems occurring frequently in practice.

In Step 3 the program consists of the given data and a sequence of instructions to be executed by the computer in a certain order for producing the answer in numeric or graphic form.

To create a good understanding of the nature of numeric work, we continue in this section with some simple general remarks.

19.1.1 Floating-Point Form of Numbers

We know that in decimal notation, every real number is represented by a finite or an infinite sequence of decimal digits. Now most computers have two ways of representing numbers, called fixed point and floating point. In a fixed-point system all numbers are given with a fixed number of decimals after the decimal point; for example, numbers given with 3 decimals are 62.358, 0.014, 1.000. In a text we would write, say, 3 decimals as 3D. Fixed-point representations are impractical in most scientific computations because of their limited range and will not concern us.

In a floating-point system we write, for instance,

\[0.1735 \times 10^{13}, \quad 0.6247 \times 10^3, \quad -0.2000 \times 10^{-10}\]

We see that in this system the number of significant digits is kept fixed, whereas the decimal point is “floating.” Here, a significant digit of a number \(c\) is any given digit of \(c\), except possibly for zeros to the left of the first nonzero digit; these zeros serve only to fix the position of the decimal point. (Thus any other zero is a significant digit of \(c\).) For instance, \(13600\), \(1.3600\), \(0.0013600\) all have 5 significant digits. In a text we indicate, say, 5 significant digits, by 5S.

The use of exponents permits us to represent very large and very small numbers. Indeed, theoretically any nonzero number \(a\) can be written as

\[a = \pm m \times 10^n, \qquad n \text{ integer.} \tag{19.1}\]

On modern computers, which use binary (base 2) numbers, \(m\) is limited to \(k\) binary digits and \(n\) is limited, giving representations (for finitely many numbers only!)

\[a = \pm m \times 2^n, \qquad 0.1 \le |m| < 1. \tag{19.2}\]

These numbers are called \(k\)-digit binary machine numbers. Their fractional part \(m\) (or mantissa) is called the mantissa. \(n\) is called the exponent of \(a\).

It is important to realize that there are only finitely many machine numbers and that they become less and less “dense” with increasing \(a\). For instance, there are as many numbers between 2 and 4 as there are between 1024 and 2048.

The smallest positive machine number \(\varepsilon\) with \(1 + \varepsilon > 1\) is called the machine accuracy. It is important to realize that there are no numbers in the intervals \([1, 1 + \varepsilon]\), \([1024, 1024 + 1024\varepsilon]\), \(\ldots\). This means that, if the mathematical answer to a computation would be \(1024 + 10^{-k}\), the computer result will be either 1024 or \(1024 + 1024\varepsilon\), so it is impossible to achieve greater accuracy.

Underflow and Overflow. The range of exponents that a typical computer can handle is very large. The IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronic Engineers) floating-point standard for single precision is from \(2^{-126}\) to \(2^{128}\) (\(1.175 \times 10^{-38}\) to \(3.403 \times 10^{38}\)) and for double precision it is from \(2^{-1022}\) to \(2^{1024}\) (\(2.225 \times 10^{-308}\) to \(1.798 \times 10^{308}\)).

If, in a computation a number outside that range occurs, this is called underflow when the number is smaller and overflow when it is larger. In the case of underflow, the result is usually set to zero and computation continues. Overflow might cause the computer to halt. Standard codes (by IMSL, NAG, etc.) are written to avoid overflow.

19.1.2 Roundoff

An error is caused by chopping (discarding all digits from some decimal on) or rounding. This error is called roundoff error, regardless of whether we chop or round. The rule for rounding off a number to \(k\) decimals is as follows.

Roundoff Rule. To round a number \(x\) to \(k\) decimals, add \(5 \times 10^{-(k+1)}\) to \(x\) and chop the digits after the \((k+1)\)st digit.

19.1.3 EXAMPLE 1 Roundoff Rule

Round the number 1.23454621 to (a) 2 decimals, (b) 3 decimals, (c) 4 decimals, (d) 5 decimals, and (e) 6 decimals.

Solution. (a) For 2 decimals we add \(5 \times 10^{-3} = 0.005\) to the given number: \[1.23454621 + 0.005 = 1.23954621\] Then we chop off the digits after the space: \(1.23\).

  1. \(1.23454621 + 0.0005 = 1.23504621\), so for 3 decimals we get 1.234.

  2. \(1.23459621\) after chopping gives us 1.2345 (4 decimals).

  3. \(1.23455121\) yields 1.23455 (5 decimals).

  4. \(1.23454671\) yields 1.234546 (6 decimals). \(\blacksquare\)

Chopping is not recommended because the corresponding error can be larger than that in rounding. (Nevertheless, some computers use it because it is simpler and faster. On the other hand, some computers and calculators improve accuracy of results by doing intermediate calculations using one or more extra digits, called guarding digits.)

Error in Rounding. Let \(\tilde{a} = \text{fl}(a)\) in (Equation eq-sec191-2) be the floating-point computer approximation of \(a\) in (Equation eq-sec191-1) obtained by rounding. Then the roundoff rule gives

\[\left| \frac{a - \tilde{a}}{a} \right| \le \frac{1}{2} \times 10^{-k}. \tag{19.3}\]

The right side is called the rounding unit \(u\). If we write \(a = \tilde{a}(1 + \delta)\), we have by algebra \(\delta = (a - \tilde{a})/\tilde{a}\); hence \(|\delta| \le u\) by (Equation eq-sec191-3). This shows that the rounding unit \(u\) is an error bound in rounding.

Rounding errors may ruin a computation completely, even a small computation. In general, these errors become the more dangerous the more arithmetic operations (perhaps several millions!) we have to perform. It is therefore important to analyze computational programs for expected rounding errors and to find an arrangement of the computations such that the effect of rounding errors is as small as possible.

Accuracy in Tables. Although available software has rendered various tables of function values superfluous, some tables (of higher functions, of coefficients of integration formulas, etc.) will still remain in occasional use. If a table shows \(k\) significant digits, it is conventionally assumed that any value in the table deviates from the exact value \(a\) by at most \(\frac{1}{2}\) unit of the \(k\)th digit.

19.1.4 Loss of Significant Digits

This means that a result of a calculation has fewer correct digits than the numbers from which it was obtained. This happens if we subtract two numbers of about the same size, for example (\(0.1439 - 0.1426\)) (“subtractive cancellation”). It may occur in simple problems, but it can be avoided in most cases by simple changes of the algorithm—if one is aware of it! Let us illustrate this with the following basic problem.

19.1.5 EXAMPLE 2 Quadratic Equation. Loss of Significant Digits

Find the roots of the equation

\[x^2 - 40x + 2 = 0\]

using 4 significant digits (abbreviated 4S) in the computation.

Solution. A formula for the roots of a quadratic equation \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) is

\[x_1 = \frac{1}{2a}(-b + \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}), \quad x_2 = \frac{1}{2a}(-b - \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}). \tag{19.4}\]

Furthermore, since \(x_1 x_2 = c/a\), another formula for those roots is

\[x_1 = \frac{c}{ax_2}, \quad x_2 = \frac{c}{ax_1} \quad \text{(as in (@eq-sec191-4)).} \tag{19.5}\]

We see that this avoids cancellation in \(x_1\) for positive \(b\).

For \(x^2 - 40x + 2 = 0\) we obtain from (Equation eq-sec191-4): \(x = -20 \pm \sqrt{398} = -20 \pm 19.95\), hence \(x_2 = -20.00 - 19.95\), involving no difficulty, and \(x_1 = -20.00 + 19.95 = -0.05\), involving loss of digits by subtractive cancellation.

In contrast, (Equation eq-sec191-5) gives \(x_1 = 2/(x_2) \approx -0.05006\), the absolute value of the error being less than one unit of the last digit, as a computation with more digits shows. The 10S-value is \(\approx -0.05006265674\). \(\blacksquare\)

19.1.6 Errors of Numeric Results

Final results of computations of unknown quantities generally are approximations; that is, they are not exact but involve errors. Such an error may result from a combination of the following effects. Roundoff errors result from rounding, as discussed above. Experimental errors are errors of given data (probably arising from measurements). Truncating errors result from truncating (prematurely breaking off), for instance, if we replace a Taylor series with the sum of its first few terms.

Formulas for Errors. If \(\tilde{a}\) is an approximate value of a quantity whose exact value is \(a\), we call the difference

\[\varepsilon = a - \tilde{a} \tag{19.6}\]

the error of \(\tilde{a}\). Hence

\[a = \tilde{a} + \varepsilon, \quad \text{True value} = \text{Approximation} + \text{Error.} \tag{19.7}\]

The relative error of \(\tilde{a}\) is defined by

\[\varepsilon_r = \frac{\varepsilon}{a} = \frac{a - \tilde{a}}{a}. \tag{19.8}\]

This looks useless because \(a\) is unknown. But if \(|\varepsilon|\) is much less than \(|\tilde{a}|\), then we can use \(\tilde{a}\) instead of \(a\) and get

\[\varepsilon_r \approx \frac{\varepsilon}{\tilde{a}}. \tag{19.9}\]

This still looks problematic because \(\varepsilon\) is unknown. But what one often can obtain in practice is an error bound for \(\varepsilon\), that is, a number \(b\) such that \(|\varepsilon| \le b\), hence \(|a - \tilde{a}| \le b\). Similarly, for the relative error, an error bound \(b_r\) such that \(|\varepsilon_r| \le b_r\), hence \(|a - \tilde{a}|/|a| \le b_r\).

19.1.7 Error Propagation

This is an important matter. It refers to how errors at the beginning and in later steps (roundoff, for example) propagate into the computation and affect accuracy, sometimes very drastically. We state here what happens to error bounds. Namely, bounds for the error add under addition and subtraction, whereas bounds for the relative error add under multiplication and division. You do well to keep this in mind.

Theorem 19.1 (Theorem 1: Error Propagation)  

  1. In addition and subtraction, a bound for the error of the results is given by the sum of the error bounds for the terms.

  2. In multiplication and division, an error bound for the relative error of the results is given (approximately) by the sum of the bounds for the relative errors of the given numbers.

PROOF (a) We use the notations \(x = \tilde{x} + \varepsilon_x\), \(y = \tilde{y} + \varepsilon_y\), \(|\varepsilon_x| \le b_x\), \(|\varepsilon_y| \le b_y\). Then for the error of the difference we obtain

\[|\varepsilon| = |x - y - (\tilde{x} - \tilde{y})| = |\varepsilon_x - \varepsilon_y| \le |\varepsilon_x| + |\varepsilon_y| \le b_x + b_y.\]

The proof for the sum is similar.

  1. For the relative error of \(\tilde{x}\tilde{y}\) we get \(|\varepsilon_r| \approx |\varepsilon_{rx}| + |\varepsilon_{ry}| \le b_{rx} + b_{ry}\). \(\blacksquare\)

19.1.8 Basic Error Principle

Every numeric method should be accompanied by an error estimate. If such a formula is lacking, is extremely complicated, or is impractical because it involves information (for instance, on derivatives) that is not available, the following may help.

Error Estimation by Comparison. Do a calculation twice with different accuracy. Regard the difference of the results as a (perhaps crude) estimate of the error of the inferior result. Indeed, by formula \(\tilde{a}_1 + \varepsilon_1 = \tilde{a}_2 + \varepsilon_2\) (\(= a\)), this implies \(\tilde{a}_2 - \tilde{a}_1 \approx \varepsilon_1\) because \(\tilde{a}_2\) is generally more accurate than \(\tilde{a}_1\), so that \(|\varepsilon_2|\) is small compared to \(|\varepsilon_1|\).

19.1.9 Algorithm. Stability

Numeric methods can be formulated as algorithms. An algorithm is a step-by-step procedure that states a numeric method in a form (a “pseudocode”) understandable to humans. The algorithm is then used to write a program in a programming language that the computer can understand so that it can execute the numeric method.

Stability. To be useful, an algorithm should be stable; that is, small changes in the initial data should cause only small changes in the final results. However, if small changes in the initial data can produce large changes in the final results, we call the algorithm unstable. This “numeric instability,” which in most cases can be avoided by choosing a better algorithm, must be distinguished from “mathematical instability” of a problem, which is called “ill-conditioning.”

19.2 Solution of Equations by Iteration

For each of the remaining sections of this chapter, we select basic kinds of problems and discuss numeric methods on how to solve them. The reader will learn about a variety of important problems and become familiar with ways of thinking in numerical analysis.

Perhaps the easiest conceptual problem is to find solutions of a single equation

\[f(x) = 0 \tag{19.10}\]

where \(f\) is a given function. A solution of (Equation eq-sec192-1) is a number \(x = s\) such that \(f(s) = 0\). Here, \(s\) suggests “solution,” but we shall also use other letters.

It is interesting to note that the task of solving (Equation eq-sec192-1) is a question made for numeric algorithms, as in general there are no direct formulas, except in a few simple cases. Examples of single equations are

\[\cosh x = \sec x, \quad \tan x = x, \quad \sin x = 0.5x, \quad x^3 + x = 1, \quad \cosh x \cos x = -1,\]

which can all be written in the form of (Equation eq-sec192-1). The first of the five equations is an algebraic equation because the corresponding \(f\) is a polynomial. In this case the solutions are called roots of the equation and the solution process is called finding roots. The other equations are transcendental equations because they involve transcendental functions.

To solve (Equation eq-sec192-1) when there is no formula for the exact solution available, we can use an approximation method, such as an iteration method. This is a method in which we start from an initial guess (which may be poor) and compute step by step (in general better and better) approximations of an unknown solution of (Equation eq-sec192-1). We discuss three such methods that are of particular practical importance.

In general, iteration methods are easy to program because the computational operations are the same in each step—just the data change from step to step—and, more importantly, if in a concrete case a method converges, it is stable in general.

19.2.1 Fixed-Point Iteration for Solving Equations

By some algebraic steps we transform (Equation eq-sec192-1) into the form

\[x = g(x). \tag{19.11}\]

Then we choose an initial guess \(x_0\) and compute

\[x_{n+1} = g(x_n) \quad (n = 0, 1, \ldots). \tag{19.12}\]

A solution of (Equation eq-sec192-2) is called a fixed point of \(g\), motivating the name of the method. This is a solution of (Equation eq-sec192-1), since from \(x = g(x)\) we can return to the original form \(f(x) = 0\). From (Equation eq-sec192-1) we may get several different forms of (Equation eq-sec192-2). The behavior of corresponding iterative sequences may differ, in particular, with respect to their speed of convergence. Indeed, some of them may not converge at all.

19.2.2 EXAMPLE 1 An Iteration Process (Fixed-Point Iteration)

Set up an iteration process for the equation \(f(x) = x^2 - 3x + 1 = 0\). Since we know the solutions \(x = 1.5 \pm 1.25\), thus \(x \approx 2.618034\) and \(x \approx 0.381966\), we can watch the behavior of the error as the iteration proceeds.

Solution. The equation may be written

\[x = g_1(x) = \frac{1}{3}(x^2 + 1), \quad \text{thus} \quad x_{n+1} = \frac{1}{3}(x_n^2 + 1). \tag{19.13}\]

If we choose \(x_0 = 1\), we obtain the sequence \(x_0 = 1.000, x_1 = 0.667, x_2 = 0.481, x_3 = 0.411, x_4 = 0.390, \ldots\), which seems to approach the smaller solution.

If we choose \(x_0 = 3\), we obtain the sequence \(x_0 = 3.000, x_1 = 3.333, x_2 = 4.037, x_3 = 5.766, x_4 = 11.415, \ldots\), which diverges.

Our equation may also be written (divide by \(x\))

\[x = g_2(x) = 3 - \frac{1}{x}, \quad \text{thus} \quad x_{n+1} = 3 - \frac{1}{x_n}, \tag{19.14}\]

and if we choose \(x_0 = 1\), we obtain the sequence \(x_0 = 1.000, x_1 = 2.000, x_2 = 2.500, x_3 = 2.600, x_4 = 2.615, \ldots\), which seems to approach the larger solution. \(\blacksquare\)

An iteration process defined by (Equation eq-sec192-3) is called convergent for an \(x_0\) if the corresponding sequence \(\{x_n\}\) is convergent. A sufficient condition for convergence is given in the following theorem.

Theorem 19.2 (Theorem 1: Convergence of Fixed-Point Iteration) Let \(s\) be a solution of \(x = g(x)\), and suppose that \(g\) has a continuous derivative in some interval \(J\) containing \(s\). Then, if \(|g'(x)| \le K < 1\) in \(J\), the iteration process defined by (Equation eq-sec192-3) converges for any \(x_0\) in \(J\). The limit of the sequence is \(s\).

PROOF By the mean value theorem of differential calculus there is a \(t\) between \(x\) and \(s\) such that \(g(x) - g(s) = g'(t)(x - s)\). Since \(g(s) = s\) and \(g(x) = x_{n+1}\), \(x = x_n\), we obtain

\[|x_{n+1} - s| = |g'(t)||x_n - s| \le K|x_n - s|.\]

Applying this inequality \(n\) times gives \(|x_n - s| \le K^n |x_0 - s|\). Since \(K < 1\), we have \(K^n \to 0\), hence \(|x_n - s| \to 0\) as \(n \to \infty\). \(\blacksquare\)

We mention that a function \(g\) satisfying the condition in Theorem 1 is called a contraction because \(|g(x) - g(v)| \le K|x - v|\) where \(K < 1\). Furthermore, \(K\) gives information on the speed of convergence. For instance, if \(K = 0.5\), then the accuracy increases by at least 2 digits in only 7 steps because \(0.5^7 < 0.01\).

19.2.3 EXAMPLE 2 An Iteration Process. Illustration of Theorem 1

Find a solution of \(f(x) = x^3 + x - 1 = 0\) by iteration.

Solution. A sketch shows that a solution lies near \(x \approx 0.57\). We may write the equation as \(x(1 + x^2) = 1\) or \(x = g_1(x) = 1/(1 + x^2)\).

Also \(|g_1'(x)| = 2|x|/(1 + x^2)^2 \le 1\) for any \(x\) because \(4x^2(1 + x^2)^2 \le (1 + x^2)^4\), so that by Theorem 1 we have convergence for any \(x_0\). Choosing \(x_0 = 1\), we obtain

\[x_1 = 0.500, \quad x_2 = 0.800, \quad x_3 = 0.610, \quad x_4 = 0.729, \quad x_5 = 0.653, \quad x_6 = 0.701, \ldots\]

The solution exact to 6D is \(s = 0.682328\). \(\blacksquare\)

19.2.4 Newton’s Method for Solving Equations

Newton’s method, also known as Newton–Raphson’s method, is another iteration method for solving equations \(f(x) = 0\) where \(f\) is assumed to have a continuous derivative \(f'\). The method is commonly used because of its simplicity and great speed.

The underlying idea is that we approximate the graph of \(f\) by suitable tangents. Using an approximate value \(x_0\) obtained from the graph of \(f\), we let \(x_1\) be the point of intersection of the \(x\)-axis and the tangent to the curve of \(f\) at \((x_0, f(x_0))\). Then

\[\tan \beta = f'(x_0) = \frac{f(x_0)}{x_0 - x_1}, \quad \text{hence} \quad x_1 = x_0 - \frac{f(x_0)}{f'(x_0)}.\]

In the second step we compute \(x_2\) from \(x_1\), in the third step \(x_3\) from \(x_2\), again by the same formula. We thus have the algorithm shown in the Table below. Formula (Equation eq-sec192-5) in this algorithm can also be obtained if we algebraically solve Taylor’s formula

\[f(x_{n+1}) \approx f(x_n) + (x_{n+1} - x_n)f'(x_n) = 0. \tag{19.15}\]

ALGORITHM NEWTON

  • INPUT: \(f\), \(f'\), initial approximation \(x_0\), tolerance \(\varepsilon > 0\), maximum number of iterations \(N\).
  • OUTPUT: Approximate solution \(x_n\) (\(n \le N\)) or message of failure.

  2. If \(f'(x_n) = 0\) then OUTPUT “Failure.” Stop.
  3. Else compute \[x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}. \tag{19.16}\]
  4. If \(|x_{n+1} - x_n| \le \varepsilon|x_{n+1}|\) then OUTPUT \(x_{n+1}\). Stop. [Procedure completed successfully]

End

  1. OUTPUT “Failure.” Stop. [Procedure completed unsuccessfully after \(N\) iterations]

19.2.8 Order of an Iteration Method. Speed of Convergence

The quality of an iteration method may be characterized by the speed of convergence. Let \(x_{n+1} = g(x_n)\) define an iteration method, and let \(x_n\) approximate a solution \(s\) of \(f(x) = 0\). Then \(x_n = s - \varepsilon_n\) where \(\varepsilon_n\) is the error of \(x_n\). Suppose that \(g\) is differentiable a number of times, so that the Taylor formula gives

\[x_{n+1} = g(x_n) = g(s) + g'(s)(x_n - s) + \frac{1}{2}g''(s)(x_n - s)^2 + \ldots \tag{19.17}\]

The exponent of \(\varepsilon_n\) in the first nonvanishing term after \(g(s)\) is called the order of the iteration process defined by \(g\). The order measures the speed of convergence. Thus

\[\varepsilon_{n+1} \approx g'(s)\varepsilon_n \quad \text{(first order)}, \quad \varepsilon_{n+1} \approx \frac{1}{2}g''(s)\varepsilon_n^2 \quad \text{(second order).} \tag{19.18}\]

Thus if \(\varepsilon_n \approx 10^{-k}\) in some step, then for second order, \(\varepsilon_{n+1} \approx c \cdot 10^{-2k}\), so that the number of significant digits is about doubled in each step.

19.2.9 Convergence of Newton’s Method

In Newton’s method, \(g(x) = x - f(x)/f'(x)\). By differentiation,

\[g'(x) = 1 - \frac{f'(x)^2 - f(x)f''(x)}{f'(x)^2} = \frac{f(x)f''(x)}{f'(x)^2}. \tag{19.19}\]

Since \(f(s) = 0\), this shows that \(g'(s) = 0\) also. Hence Newton’s method is at least of second order. If we differentiate again and set \(x = s\), we find

\[g''(s) = \frac{f''(s)}{f'(s)} \tag{19.20}\]

which will not be zero in general. This proves:

Theorem 19.3 (Theorem 2: Second-Order Convergence of Newton’s Method) If \(f\) is three times differentiable and \(f'(s)\) and \(f''(s)\) are not zero at a solution \(s\) of \(f(x) = 0\), then for \(x_0\) sufficiently close to \(s\), Newton’s method is of second order.

For Newton’s method, (Equation eq-sec192-7) becomes, by (Equation eq-sec192-8star),

\[\varepsilon_{n+1} \approx -\frac{f''(s)}{2f'(s)} \varepsilon_n^2. \tag{19.21}\]

19.2.10 EXAMPLE 6 Prior Error Estimate of the Number of Newton Iteration Steps

Use \(x_1 = 1.901\) and \(x_0 = 2\) in Example 4 for estimating how many iteration steps we need to produce the solution to 5D-accuracy.

Solution. We have \(f(x) = x - 2\sin x = 0\). Differentiation gives \(f''(x)/[2f'(x)] \approx f''(x_1)/[2f'(x_1)] = -2\sin x_1/[2(1 - 2\cos x_1)] \approx 0.57\). Hence (Equation eq-sec192-9) gives \(|\varepsilon_{n+1}| \approx 0.57|\varepsilon_n|^2\). We show below that \(|\varepsilon_0| \approx 0.11\). Consequently, our condition \(0.57M^{2^n-1}|\varepsilon_0|^{2^n} < 5 \times 10^{-6}\) becomes satisfied for \(n = 2\), in good agreement with Example 4. \(\blacksquare\)

19.2.11 Secant Method for Solving \(f(x) = 0\)

Newton’s method is very powerful but has the disadvantage that the derivative may sometimes be a far more difficult expression than \(f\) itself and its evaluation therefore computationally expensive. This situation suggests the idea of replacing the derivative with the difference quotient

\[f'(x_n) \approx \frac{f(x_n) - f(x_{n-1})}{x_n - x_{n-1}}.\]

Then instead of (Equation eq-sec192-5) we have the formula of the popular secant method

\[x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n) \cdot \frac{x_n - x_{n-1}}{f(x_n) - f(x_{n-1})}. \tag{19.22}\]

Geometrically, we intersect the \(x\)-axis with the secant of \(y = f(x)\) passing through \((x_{n-1}, f(x_{n-1}))\) and \((x_n, f(x_n))\). We need two starting values \(x_0\) and \(x_1\). Evaluation of derivatives is now avoided. It can be shown that convergence is superlinear (that is, more rapid than linear), almost quadratic like Newton’s method. The algorithm is similar to that of Newton’s method.

CAUTION! It is not good to write (Equation eq-sec192-10) as \(x_{n+1} = [x_{n-1}f(x_n) - x_n f(x_{n-1})]/[f(x_n) - f(x_{n-1})]\) because this may lead to loss of significant digits if \(f(x_n)\) and \(f(x_{n-1})\) are about equal.

19.3 Interpolation

Interpolation concerns the problem of finding function values at points that lie between given data points. The given data may come from measurements or from a table, and we want an approximation formula which gives values at other points.

Problem of Polynomial Interpolation. Given \(n + 1\) points \((x_0, f_0), (x_1, f_1), \ldots, (x_n, f_n)\) with distinct \(x_j\), find a polynomial \(p_n(x)\) of degree at most \(n\) such that

\[p_n(x_j) = f_j \quad (j = 0, 1, \ldots, n). \tag{19.23}\]

Uniqueness. The interpolation polynomial satisfying (Equation eq-sec193-1) is unique. For proof, suppose there are two polynomials \(p_n\) and \(q_n\) satisfying (Equation eq-sec193-1). Then \(d(x) = p_n(x) - q_n(x)\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(n\) with \(n + 1\) zeros, hence \(d \equiv 0\).

19.3.1 Linear Interpolation

The simplest case is \(n = 1\), which gives the linear interpolation polynomial

\[p_1(x) = f_0 + (x - x_0)\frac{f_1 - f_0}{x_1 - x_0}.\]

19.3.2 EXAMPLE 1 Linear Interpolation

From the table \(\ln 9.0 = 2.1972\) and \(\ln 9.5 = 2.2513\), find \(\ln 9.2\) by linear interpolation.

Solution.

\[p_1(9.2) = 2.1972 + (9.2 - 9.0)\frac{2.2513 - 2.1972}{9.5 - 9.0} = 2.1972 + 0.2 \cdot 0.1082 = 2.2189.\]

The true value is \(\ln 9.2 = 2.2192\) (4D), so the error is \(\approx 0.0003\). \(\blacksquare\)

19.3.3 Lagrange Interpolation Polynomial

The Lagrange form of the interpolation polynomial is

\[p_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} L_k(x) f_k, \tag{19.24}\]

where the Lagrange basis polynomials \(L_k(x) = l_k(x)/l_k(x_k)\) satisfy \(L_k(x_k) = 1\) and \(L_k(x_j) = 0\) for \(j \ne k\), and are given by

\[l_k(x) = (x - x_0)\cdots(x - x_{k-1})(x - x_{k+1})\cdots(x - x_n), \quad k = 0, 1, \ldots, n. \tag{19.25}\]

19.3.4 EXAMPLE 2 Quadratic Interpolation

Compute \(\ln 9.2\) from \(\ln 9.0 = 2.1972\), \(\ln 9.5 = 2.2513\), \(\ln 11.0 = 2.3979\) by Lagrange quadratic interpolation.

Solution. We use the three Lagrange basis polynomials \(L_0, L_1, L_2\) for \(x_0 = 9.0\), \(x_1 = 9.5\), \(x_2 = 11.0\):

\[L_0(9.2) = \frac{(9.2 - 9.5)(9.2 - 11.0)}{(9.0 - 9.5)(9.0 - 11.0)} = \frac{(-0.3)(-1.8)}{(-0.5)(-2.0)} = 0.5400\]

\[L_1(9.2) = \frac{(9.2 - 9.0)(9.2 - 11.0)}{(9.5 - 9.0)(9.5 - 11.0)} = \frac{(0.2)(-1.8)}{(0.5)(-1.5)} = 0.4800\]

\[L_2(9.2) = \frac{(9.2 - 9.0)(9.2 - 9.5)}{(11.0 - 9.0)(11.0 - 9.5)} = \frac{(0.2)(-0.3)}{(2.0)(1.5)} = -0.0200\]

So \(\ln 9.2 \approx 0.5400 \times 2.1972 + 0.4800 \times 2.2513 + (-0.0200) \times 2.3979 = 2.2192\). \(\blacksquare\)

19.3.5 Error Estimate

It can be shown that if \(f^{(n+1)}\) exists and is continuous, then for a suitable \(t\) between \(x_0\) and \(x_n\) (or between \(\min(x_0, \ldots, x_n, x)\) and \(\max(x_0, \ldots, x_n, x)\) if we extrapolate),

\[P_n(x) = f(x) - p_n(x) = (x - x_0)(x - x_1)\cdots(x - x_n)\frac{f^{(n+1)}(t)}{(n+1)!}. \tag{19.26}\]

Theorem 19.4 (Theorem 1: Error of Interpolation) Formula (Equation eq-sec193-5) gives the error for any polynomial interpolation method if \(f\) has a continuous \((n+1)\)st derivative.

\(t = 9.5\) gives the maximum and \(t = 9\) gives the minimum, so \(0.00033 \le P_1(9.2) \le 0.00037\).

  1. Estimation by the Error Principle. We calculate \(p_1(9.2) = 2.21885\) as before and then \(p_2(9.2) = 2.21916\) (from Example 2 with 5D). The difference \(p_2(9.2) - p_1(9.2) = 0.00031\) is the approximate error of \(p_1\). \(\blacksquare\)

19.3.7 Newton’s Divided Difference Interpolation

For given data the interpolation polynomial satisfying (Equation eq-sec193-1) is unique. But for different purposes we may use \(p_n\) in different forms. Newton’s forms are practically more important because they involve fewer arithmetic operations than Lagrange’s form. Moreover, it often happens that we have to increase the degree \(n\) to reach a required accuracy. Then in Newton’s forms we can use all the previous work and just add another term.

The divided differences are defined recursively:

\[f[x_0] = f_0, \quad f[x_j, x_{j+1}] = \frac{f_{j+1} - f_j}{x_{j+1} - x_j}\]

and in general

\[f[x_j, \ldots, x_{j+m}] = \frac{f[x_{j+1}, \ldots, x_{j+m}] - f[x_j, \ldots, x_{j+m-1}]}{x_{j+m} - x_j}. \tag{19.27}\]

Newton’s divided difference interpolation formula is

\[\begin{aligned} f(x) \approx f_0 &+ (x - x_0)f[x_0, x_1] + (x - x_0)(x - x_1)f[x_0, x_1, x_2] + \cdots \\ &+ (x - x_0)(x - x_1)\cdots(x - x_{n-1})f[x_0, \ldots, x_n]. \end{aligned} \tag{19.28}\]

\(x_j\) \(f_j\) \(f[x_j, x_{j+1}]\) \(f[x_j, x_{j+1}, x_{j+2}]\) \(f[x_j, \ldots, x_{j+3}]\)

At \(x = 9.2\):

\[f(9.2) \approx 2.079442 + 0.117783(1.2) + (-0.006433)(1.2)(0.2) + 0.000411(1.2)(0.2)(-0.3) = 2.219208.\]

19.3.9 Equal Spacing: Newton’s Forward Difference Formula

Newton’s divided difference formula (Equation eq-sec193-10) is valid for arbitrarily spaced nodes. However, in many applications the \(x\)’s are regularly spaced—for instance, in measurements taken at regular intervals of time. Then, denoting the distance by \(h\), we can write

\[x_0, \quad x_1 = x_0 + h, \quad x_2 = x_0 + 2h, \quad \ldots, \quad x_n = x_0 + nh. \tag{19.29}\]

The \(k\)th forward difference of \(f\) at \(x_j\) is defined by

\[\Delta f_j = f_{j+1} - f_j, \quad \Delta^2 f_j = \Delta f_{j+1} - \Delta f_j, \quad \Delta^k f_j = \Delta^{k-1} f_{j+1} - \Delta^{k-1} f_j. \tag{19.30}\]

It can be shown that if we have regular spacing (Equation eq-sec193-11), then

\[f[x_0, \ldots, x_k] = \frac{1}{k! h^k} \Delta^k f_0. \tag{19.31}\]

With this and (Equation eq-sec193-13), formula (Equation eq-sec193-10) becomes Newton’s (Gregory–Newton’s) forward difference interpolation formula (setting \(x = x_0 + rh\), \(r = (x - x_0)/h\)):

\[f(x) \approx p_n(x) = \sum_{s=0}^{n} \binom{r}{s} \Delta^s f_0 = f_0 + r\Delta f_0 + \frac{r(r-1)}{2!}\Delta^2 f_0 + \cdots + \frac{r(r-1)\cdots(r-n+1)}{n!}\Delta^n f_0. \tag{19.32}\]

The error from (Equation eq-sec193-5) is

\[P_n(x) = f(x) - p_n(x) = \frac{h^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} r(r-1)\cdots(r-n) f^{(n+1)}(t). \tag{19.33}\]

19.3.11 Equal Spacing: Newton’s Backward Difference Formula

Instead of forward-sloping differences we may also employ backward-sloping differences. The \(k\)th backward difference of \(f\) at \(x_j\) is defined by

\[\nabla f_j = f_j - f_{j-1}, \quad \nabla^2 f_j = \nabla f_j - \nabla f_{j-1}, \quad \nabla^k f_j = \nabla^{k-1} f_j - \nabla^{k-1} f_{j-1}. \tag{19.34}\]

A formula similar to (Equation eq-sec193-14) but involving backward differences is Newton’s (Gregory–Newton’s) backward difference interpolation formula:

\[f(x) \approx p_n(x) = \sum_{s=0}^{n} \binom{r+s-1}{s} \nabla^s f_0, \quad x = x_0 + rh. \tag{19.35}\]

19.4 Spline Interpolation

Given data \((x_0, f_0), (x_1, f_1), \ldots, (x_n, f_n)\) can be interpolated by a polynomial \(P_n(x)\) of degree \(n\) or less so that the curve of \(P_n\) passes through these points (\(f_j = f(x_j)\)). See sec-interpolation.

Now if \(n\) is large, there may be trouble: \(P_n(x)\) may tend to oscillate for \(x\) between the nodes \(x_0, \ldots, x_n\). Hence we must be prepared for numeric instability (sec-introduction). A famous example by C. Runge shows that the maximum error even approaches \(\infty\) as \(n \to \infty\) (with the nodes kept equidistant and their number increased). Those undesirable oscillations are avoided by the method of splines initiated by I. J. Schoenberg in 1946. This method is widely used in practice. It also laid the foundation for much of modern CAD (computer-aided design). Its name is borrowed from a draftman’s spline, which is an elastic rod bent to pass through given points and held in place by weights.

Instead of using a single high-degree polynomial over the entire interval \([x_0, x_n]\):

\[a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n = b \tag{19.36}\]

we use \(n\) low-degree, e.g., cubic, polynomials \(q_0(x), q_1(x), \ldots, q_{n-1}(x)\), one over each subinterval between adjacent nodes, hence from \(x_0\) to \(x_1\), then from \(x_1\) to \(x_2\), and so on. From this we compose an interpolation function called a spline:

\[g(x_0) = f_0, \quad g(x_1) = f_1, \quad \ldots, \quad g(x_n) = f_n. \tag{19.37}\]

Thus spline interpolation is piecewise polynomial interpolation.

We shall consider cubic splines because these are the most important ones in applications. By definition, a cubic spline \(g(x)\) interpolating given data \((x_0, f_0), \ldots, (x_n, f_n)\) is a continuous function on the interval \([x_0, x_n]\) that has continuous first and second derivatives and satisfies the interpolation condition (Equation eq-sec194-2); furthermore, between adjacent nodes, \(g(x)\) is given by a polynomial \(q_j(x)\) of degree 3 or less.

We claim that there is such a cubic spline. And if in addition to (Equation eq-sec194-2) we also require that

\[g'(x_0) = k_0, \quad g'(x_n) = k_n \tag{19.38}\]

(given tangent directions of \(g\) at the two endpoints of the interval), then we have a uniquely determined cubic spline.

Theorem 19.5 (Theorem 1: Existence and Uniqueness of Cubic Splines) Let \((x_0, f_0), (x_1, f_1), \ldots, (x_n, f_n)\) with given (arbitrarily spaced) \(x_j\) see ( eq-sec194-1 and given \(f_j = f(x_j)\), \(j = 0, 1, \ldots, n\). Let \(k_0\) and \(k_n\) be any given numbers. Then there is one and only one cubic spline \(g(x)\) corresponding to (Equation eq-sec194-1) and satisfying (Equation eq-sec194-2) and (Equation eq-sec194-3).

PROOF By definition, on every subinterval \([x_j, x_{j+1}]\), the spline must agree with a polynomial \(q_j(x)\) of degree not exceeding 3 such that

\[q_j(x_j) = f(x_j), \quad q_j(x_{j+1}) = f(x_{j+1}) \quad (j = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1). \tag{19.39}\]

For the derivatives we write

\[q_j'(x_j) = k_j, \quad q_j'(x_{j+1}) = k_{j+1} \quad (j = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1). \tag{19.40}\]

with \(k_0\) and \(k_n\) given and \(k_1, \ldots, k_{n-1}\) to be determined later. Using the notation \(c_j = 1/h_j = 1/(x_{j+1} - x_j)\), we can verify that the unique cubic polynomial satisfying (Equation eq-sec194-4) and (Equation eq-sec194-5) is

\[\begin{aligned} q_j(x) = & f(x_j)c_j^2(x - x_{j+1})^2[1 + 2c_j(x - x_j)] + f(x_{j+1})c_j^2(x - x_j)^2[1 - 2c_j(x - x_{j+1})] \\ & + k_j c_j^2(x - x_j)(x - x_{j+1})^2 + k_{j+1}c_j^2(x - x_j)^2(x - x_{j+1}). \end{aligned} \tag{19.41}\]

By definition, \(g(x)\) has continuous second derivatives. This gives the conditions \((j = 1, \ldots, n-1)\):

\[c_{j-1}k_{j-1} + 2(c_{j-1} + c_j)k_j + c_jk_{j+1} = 3[c_{j-1}^2 \nabla f_j + c_j^2 \nabla f_{j+1}]. \tag{19.42}\]

This linear system of \(n-1\) equations has a unique solution since the coefficient matrix is strictly diagonally dominant. Hence the determinant of the matrix cannot be zero, so that we may determine unique values of the first derivative of \(g\) at the nodes. This proves the theorem. \(\blacksquare\)

Storage and Time Demands in solving (Equation eq-sec194-9) are modest, since the matrix of (Equation eq-sec194-9) is sparse (has few nonzero entries) and tridiagonal (may have nonzero entries only on the diagonal and on the two adjacent “parallels” above and below it). Pivoting is not necessary because of that dominance.

Condition (Equation eq-sec194-3) includes the clamped conditions

\[g'(x_0) = f'(x_0), \quad g'(x_n) = f'(x_n), \tag{19.43}\]

in which the tangent directions at the ends are given. Other conditions of practical interest are the free or natural conditions

\[g''(x_0) = 0, \quad g''(x_n) = 0, \tag{19.44}\]

(geometrically: zero curvature at the ends, as for the draftman’s spline), giving a natural spline.

Determination of Splines. Let \(k_0\) and \(k_n\) be given. Obtain \(k_1, \ldots, k_{n-1}\) by solving the linear system (Equation eq-sec194-9). The spline to be found consists of \(n\) cubic polynomials. We write these polynomials in the form

\[q_j(x) = a_{j0} + a_{j1}(x - x_j) + a_{j2}(x - x_j)^2 + a_{j3}(x - x_j)^3. \tag{19.45}\]

The coefficients are:

\[\begin{aligned} a_{j0} &= f_j, \quad a_{j1} = k_j, \\ a_{j2} &= \frac{3}{h_j^2}(f_{j+1} - f_j) - \frac{1}{h_j}(k_{j+1} + 2k_j), \\ a_{j3} &= \frac{2}{h_j^3}(f_j - f_{j+1}) + \frac{1}{h_j^2}(k_{j+1} + k_j). \end{aligned} \tag{19.46}\]

Note that for equidistant nodes of distance \(h\), we can write \(c_j = c = 1/h\) in and have from (Equation eq-sec194-9) simply

\[k_{j-1} + 4k_j + k_{j+1} = \frac{3}{h}(f_{j+1} - f_{j-1}) \quad (j = 1, \ldots, n-1). \tag{19.47}\]

19.5 Numeric Integration and Differentiation

In applications, the engineer often encounters integrals that are very difficult or even impossible to solve analytically. For example, the error function, the Fresnel integrals, and others cannot be evaluated by the usual methods of calculus. We then need methods from numerical analysis to evaluate such integrals.

Numeric integration means the numeric evaluation of integrals

\[J = \int_a^b f(x) \, dx\]

where \(a\) and \(b\) are given and \(f\) is a function given analytically by a formula or empirically by a table of values. Geometrically, \(J\) is the area under the curve of \(f\) between \(a\) and \(b\), taken with a minus sign where \(f\) is negative.

19.5.1 Rectangular Rule. Trapezoidal Rule

Numeric integration methods are obtained by approximating the integrand \(f\) by functions that can easily be integrated.

The simplest formula, the rectangular rule, is obtained if we subdivide the interval of integration \([a, b]\) into \(n\) subintervals of equal length \(h = (b-a)/n\) and in each subinterval approximate \(f\) by the constant \(f(x_j^*)\), the value of \(f\) at the midpoint of the \(j\)th subinterval. Then

\[J = \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \approx h[f(x_1^*) + f(x_2^*) + \cdots + f(x_n^*)]. \tag{19.49}\]

The trapezoidal rule is generally more accurate. We take the same subdivision as before and approximate \(f\) by a broken line of segments (chords) with endpoints on the curve of \(f\). Then the area under the curve of \(f\) between \(a\) and \(b\) is approximated by \(n\) trapezoids, giving the trapezoidal rule

\[J = \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \approx h\left[\frac{1}{2}f(a) + f(x_1) + f(x_2) + \cdots + f(x_{n-1}) + \frac{1}{2}f(b)\right]. \tag{19.50}\]

19.5.5 Simpson’s Rule of Integration

Piecewise constant approximation of \(f\) led to the rectangular rule (Equation eq-sec195-1), piecewise linear approximation to the trapezoidal rule (Equation eq-sec195-2), and piecewise quadratic approximation will lead to Simpson’s rule, which is of great practical importance because it is sufficiently accurate for most problems, but still sufficiently simple.

To derive Simpson’s rule, we divide the interval of integration \([a, b]\) into an even number of equal subintervals \(2m\), with length \(h = (b-a)/(2m)\), with endpoints \(x_0 (= a), x_1, \ldots, x_{2m-1}, x_{2m} (= b)\). We approximate \(f\) over consecutive pairs of subintervals by quadratic Lagrange polynomials, then integrate. Summing all \(m\) such formulas we obtain Simpson’s rule:

\[J = \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \approx \frac{h}{3}(f_0 + 4f_1 + 2f_2 + 4f_3 + \cdots + 2f_{2m-2} + 4f_{2m-1} + f_{2m}). \tag{19.54}\]

where \(h = (b-a)/(2m)\) and \(f_j = f(x_j)\).

Table 19.4: Simpson’s Rule of Integration

ALGORITHM SIMPSON (\(a, b, m, f_0, f_1, \ldots, f_{2m}\))

This algorithm computes the integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) from given values \(f_j = f(x_j)\) at equidistant \(x_0 = a, x_1 = x_0 + h, \ldots, x_{2m} = x_0 + 2mh = b\) by Simpson’s rule (Equation eq-sec195-7), where \(h = (b-a)/(2m)\).

  • INPUT: \(a, b, m, f_0, \ldots, f_{2m}\)
  • OUTPUT: Approximate value \(\tilde{J}\) of \(J\)

Compute \(s_1 = f_1 + f_3 + \cdots + f_{2m-1}\), \(s_2 = f_2 + f_4 + \cdots + f_{2m-2}\), \(s_0 = f_0 + f_{2m}\), \(h = (b-a)/(2m)\).

OUTPUT \(\tilde{J} = \frac{h}{3}(s_0 + 4s_1 + 2s_2)\). Stop.

End SIMPSON

Error of Simpson’s Rule. If the fourth derivative \(f^{(4)}\) exists and is continuous on \([a, b]\), the error \(P_S\) of (Equation eq-sec195-7) is

\[P_S = -\frac{(b-a)^5}{180(2m)^4}f^{(4)}(\hat{t}) = -\frac{b-a}{180}h^4 f^{(4)}(\hat{t}). \tag{19.55}\]

Error Bounds:

\[CM_4 \le P_S \le CM_4^* \quad \text{where} \quad C = -\frac{b-a}{180}h^4. \tag{19.56}\]

Degree of Precision (DP) of an integration formula. This is the maximum degree of arbitrary polynomials for which the formula gives exact values. For Simpson’s rule, \(\text{DP} = 3\) because \(f^{(4)}\) is identically zero for a cubic polynomial. This makes Simpson’s rule sufficiently accurate for most practical problems and accounts for its popularity.

Error Estimation by Halving \(h\):

\[P_{h/2} \approx \frac{1}{15}(J_{h/2} - J_h). \tag{19.57}\]

19.5.8 Adaptive Integration

The idea is to adapt step \(h\) to the variability of \(f\). That is, where \(f\) varies but little, we can proceed in large steps without causing a substantial error in the integral, but where \(f\) varies rapidly, we have to take small steps in order to stay everywhere close enough to the curve of \(f\).

Changing \(h\) is done systematically, usually by halving \(h\), and automatically (not “by hand”) depending on the size of the (estimated) error over a subinterval. The subinterval is halved if the corresponding error is still too large, that is, larger than a given tolerance TOL (maximum admissible absolute error), or is not halved if the error is less than or equal to TOL.

19.5.11 Gauss Integration Formulas. Maximum Degree of Precision

Our integration formulas discussed so far use function values at predetermined (equidistant) \(x\)-values (nodes) and give exact results for polynomials not exceeding a certain degree. But we can get much more accurate integration formulas by setting

\[\int_{-1}^{1} f(t) \, dt \approx \sum_{j=1}^{n} A_j f(t_j) \tag{19.58}\]

with fixed \(n\), and \(f_j = f(t_j)\) obtained by setting \(x = \frac{1}{2}[a(t-1) + b(t+1)]\). Then we determine the \(n\) coefficients \(A_j\) and \(n\) nodes \(t_j\) so that (Equation eq-sec195-11) gives exact results for polynomials of degree \(k\) as high as possible.

Gauss has shown that exactness for polynomials of degree not exceeding \(2n - 1\) (instead of \(n - 1\) for predetermined nodes) can be attained. The nodes \(t_j\) are the \(j\)th zero of the Legendre polynomial \(P_n\) () and the coefficients \(A_j\) which depend on \(n\) but not on \(f\), are obtained by using Lagrange’s interpolation polynomial. Formula (Equation eq-sec195-11) is called a Gauss integration formula or Gauss quadrature formula. Its degree of precision is \(2n - 1\).

19.5.14 Numeric Differentiation

Numeric differentiation is the computation of values of the derivative of a function \(f\) from given values of \(f\). Numeric differentiation should be avoided whenever possible. Whereas integration is a smoothing process and is not very sensitive to small inaccuracies in function values, differentiation tends to make matters rough and generally gives values that are much less accurate than those of \(f\).

We use the notations \(f_j' = f'(x_j)\), \(f_j'' = f''(x_j)\), etc., and may obtain rough approximation formulas for derivatives by remembering that

\[f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}.\]

This suggests

\[f_{1/2}' \approx \frac{\Delta f_1}{h} = \frac{f_1 - f_0}{h}. \tag{19.59}\]

Similarly, for the second derivative we obtain

\[f_1'' \approx \frac{\delta^2 f_1}{h^2} = \frac{f_2 - 2f_1 + f_0}{h^2}, \quad \text{etc.} \tag{19.60}\]

More accurate approximations are obtained by differentiating suitable Lagrange polynomials. Differentiating in sec-interpolation and evaluating at \(x_0, x_1, x_2\), we obtain the “three-point formulas”:

\[f_0' \approx \frac{1}{2h}(-3f_0 + 4f_1 - f_2) \tag{19.61}\]

\[f_1' \approx \frac{1}{2h}(-f_0 + f_2) \tag{19.62}\]

\[f_2' \approx \frac{1}{2h}(f_0 - 4f_1 + 3f_2) \tag{19.63}\]

Applying the same idea to the Lagrange polynomial \(p_4(x)\) we obtain similar formulas, in particular,

\[f_2' \approx \frac{1}{12h}(f_0 - 8f_1 + 8f_3 - f_4). \tag{19.64}\]

19.7 Summary of Numerics in General

In this chapter we discussed concepts that are relevant throughout numeric work as a whole and methods of a general nature, as opposed to methods for linear algebra () or differential equations ().

In scientific computations we use the floating-point representation of numbers (sec-introduction); fixed-point representation is less suitable in most cases.

Numeric methods give approximate values of quantities. The error of \(\tilde{a}\) is

\[\varepsilon = a - \tilde{a} \quad (@sec-introduction)\]

where \(a\) is the exact value. The relative error of \(\tilde{a}\) is \(\varepsilon/a\). Errors arise from rounding, inaccuracy of measured values, truncation (that is, replacement of integrals by sums, series by partial sums), and so on.

An algorithm is called numerically stable if small changes in the initial data give only correspondingly small changes in the final results. Unstable algorithms are generally useless because errors may become so large that results will be very inaccurate. The numeric instability of algorithms must not be confused with the mathematical instability of problems (“ill-conditioned problems,” sec-solution-equations).

Fixed-point iteration is a method for solving equations \(f(x) = 0\) in which the equation is first transformed algebraically to \(x = g(x)\), an initial guess \(x_0\) for the solution is made, and then approximations are successively computed by iteration from (see sec-solution-equations)

\[x_{n+1} = g(x_n) \quad (n = 0, 1, \ldots). \tag{19.65}\]

Newton’s method for solving equations is an iteration

\[x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} \quad (@sec-solution-equations). \tag{19.66}\]

Here \(x_{n+1}\) is the \(x\)-intercept of the tangent of the curve \(y = f(x)\) at the point \((x_n, f(x_n))\). This method is of second order (Theorem 2, sec-solution-equations). If we replace \(f'(x_n)\) in (3) by a difference quotient (geometrically: we replace the tangent by a secant), we obtain the secant method; see (Equation eq-sec192-10) in sec-solution-equations.

Polynomial interpolation means the determination of a polynomial \(p_n(x)\) of degree \(n\) (or less) such that \(p_n(x_j) = f_j\), where \((x_0, f_0), \ldots, (x_n, f_n)\) are measured or observed values, values of a function, etc. \(p_n(x)\) is called an interpolation polynomial. For data, \(p_n(x)\) of degree \(n\) (or less) is unique. However, it can be written in different forms, notably in Lagrange’s form (Equation eq-sec193-3a), sec-interpolation, or in Newton’s divided difference form (Equation eq-sec193-10), sec-interpolation, which requires fewer operations. For regularly spaced \(x_0, x_1 = x_0 + h, \ldots, x_n = x_0 + nh\), the latter becomes Newton’s forward difference formula (formula (Equation eq-sec193-14) in sec-interpolation):

\[f(x) \approx p_n(x) = f_0 + r\Delta f_0 + \cdots + \frac{r(r-1)\cdots(r-n+1)}{n!}\Delta^n f_0 \quad (x = x_0 + rh, \; r = (x - x_0)/h) \tag{19.67}\]

where \(\Delta f_j = f_{j+1} - f_j\) and \(\Delta^k f_j = \Delta^{k-1} f_{j+1} - \Delta^{k-1} f_j\) (\(k = 2, 3, \ldots\)).

Interpolation polynomials may become numerically unstable as \(n\) increases, and instead of interpolating and approximating by a single high-degree polynomial it is preferable to use a cubic spline \(g(x)\), that is, a twice continuously differentiable interpolation function [thus, \(g(x_j) = f_j\)], which in each subinterval \([x_j, x_{j+1}]\) consists of a cubic polynomial \(q_j(x)\); see sec-spline-interpolation.

Simpson’s rule of numeric integration is see ( eq-sec195-7

\[\int_a^b f(x) \, dx \approx \frac{h}{3}(f_0 + 4f_1 + 2f_2 + 4f_3 + \cdots + 2f_{2m-2} + 4f_{2m-1} + f_{2m}) \tag{19.68}\]

with equally spaced nodes \(x_j = x_0 + jh\), \(j = 1, \ldots, 2m\), \(h = (b-a)/(2m)\), and \(f_j = f(x_j)\). It is simple but accurate enough for many applications. Its degree of precision is \(\text{DP} = 3\) because the error (Equation eq-sec195-8), sec-numeric-integration, involves \(h^4\). A more practical error estimate is (Equation eq-sec195-10), sec-numeric-integration,

\[P_{h/2} \approx \frac{1}{15}(J_{h/2} - J_h),\]

obtained by first computing \(J\) with step \(h\), then with step \(h/2\), and then taking \(\frac{1}{15}\) of the difference of the results.

Simpson’s rule is the most important of the Newton–Cotes formulas, which are obtained by integrating Lagrange interpolation polynomials, linear ones for the trapezoidal rule (Equation eq-sec195-2), sec-numeric-integration, quadratic for Simpson’s rule, cubic for the three-eighths rule, etc.

Adaptive integration (sec-numeric-integration, Example 6) is integration that adjusts (“adapts”) the step automatically to the variability of \(f(x)\).

Romberg integration (Team Project 26, sec-ps19-5) starts from the trapezoidal rule (Equation eq-sec195-2), sec-numeric-integration, with \(h, h/2, h/4\), etc. and improves results by systematically adding error estimates.

Gauss integration (Equation eq-sec195-11), sec-numeric-integration, is important because of its great accuracy compared to Newton–Cotes (\(\text{DP} = 2n - 1\) compared to \(\text{DP} = n - 1\) or \(n\)). This is achieved by an optimal choice of the nodes, which are not equally spaced; see Table tbl-gauss-integration, sec-numeric-integration.
